Understanding Email Authentication: A Complete Guide to SPF, DKIM, and DMARC

Published by Jayaram Sappa • Lead Security Researcher • Updated October 2026

1. The Problem: SMTP Was Born Without Authentication

The Simple Mail Transfer Protocol (SMTP), originally standardized in RFC 821 in 1982, was designed for an academic research network where all hosts were trusted. An SMTP client could connect to any receiving Mail Transfer Agent (MTA) and specify any arbitrary address in the MAIL FROM and From: fields.

To remedy this fundamental architectural vulnerability, three interlocking protocols were developed over the subsequent decades:

  • SPF (Sender Policy Framework, RFC 7208): Validates that the connecting sending IP address is authorized by the domain owner to transmit messages.
  • DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail, RFC 6376): Asserts cryptographic integrity and sender authenticity via public key cryptography and digital signatures.
  • DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance, RFC 7489): Enforces alignment between the visible From address and the underlying SPF/DKIM domains, defining handling policies for failures.

2. Sender Policy Framework (SPF) Deep Dive

SPF operates by publishing a TXT DNS record on the sender's sending domain. When a recipient MTA receives an inbound connection, it queries the domain listed in the envelope sender (the Return-Path or MAIL FROM address).

Example SPF TXT Record: 
v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ip4:198.51.100.0/24 -all

SPF Mechanisms and Qualifiers

  • + (Pass): The IP is explicitly authorized (default qualifier if omitted).
  • - (Fail / HardFail): The IP is explicitly unauthorized. MTAs are instructed to reject.
  • ~ (SoftFail): The IP is likely unauthorized; MTAs should accept but flag as suspicious or place in spam.
  • ? (Neutral): No assertion is made regarding the IP address.

The 10-DNS Lookup Limit: To prevent denial-of-service amplification attacks, RFC 7208 limits SPF evaluation to a maximum of 10 recursive DNS lookups (e.g., include, a, mx, redirect). If this threshold is exceeded, the receiving MTA produces a permerror, causing SPF verification to fail.

3. DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM)

Unlike SPF, which verifies the sending server's IP address, DKIM verifies that the email content itself has not been tampered with in transit and was authorized by the signer.

Example DKIM-Signature Header: 
DKIM-Signature: v=1; a=rsa-sha256; c=relaxed/relaxed; d=tracexmail.com;
  s=20261001; t=1728472800;
  h=from:to:subject:date:message-id:content-type;
  bh=47DEQpj8HBSa+/TImW+5JCeuQeRkm5NMpJWZG3hSuFU=;
  b=dBz8K9...[cryptographic signature]...

Key DKIM Parameters

  • d= (Domain): The domain asserting responsibility for the message.
  • s= (Selector): The selector namespace where the recipient MTA queries the public key in DNS (e.g., selector._domainkey.domain.com).
  • c= (Canonicalization): Algorithm for handling whitespace and line breaks before hashing (e.g., simple or relaxed).
  • bh= (Body Hash): Base64-encoded SHA-256 hash of the canonicalized email body.
  • b= (Signature): Digital signature calculated across the header list specified in h=.

4. DMARC: The Crucial Alignment Layer

A malicious actor can easily pass SPF using their own rogue domain (e.g., attacker.com) while spoofing the visible RFC 5322 From: ceo@victim.com. SPF alone does not inspect the visible From: header.

This is where DMARC bridges the gap through Identifier Alignment:

  • SPF Alignment: The domain in the visible From: header must match (or share an organizational root with) the domain in the envelope Return-Path.
  • DKIM Alignment: The domain in the visible From: header must match the d= domain specified in a valid DKIM signature.
Example DMARC TXT Record: 
v=DMARC1; p=reject; sp=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-rua@tracexmail.com; aspf=s; adkim=r; pct=100

DMARC Policy Directives (p=)

  • p=none: Monitoring mode. Receiving MTAs deliver failing messages normally but send aggregate reports.
  • p=quarantine: Instructs recipient MTAs to route non-aligned messages to the spam/junk folder.
  • p=reject: The gold standard of zero-trust email defense. Non-aligned messages are dropped at the SMTP handshake level.

5. Authenticated Received Chain (ARC - RFC 8617)

When an email is forwarded through an intermediary (such as an email discussion list, ticketing system, or security gateway), the sender IP changes (breaking SPF) and headers may be modified (breaking DKIM).

ARC provides an immutable cryptographic chain of custody across intermediate forwarders, allowing receiving servers to verify that the message authenticated successfully upon its initial ingress.
