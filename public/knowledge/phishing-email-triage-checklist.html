Phishing Email Triage Checklist: 10-Step SOC Analyst Workflow

Published by the TraceXMail team • Updated October 23, 2026

1. The Importance of Standardized Phishing Triage

In an enterprise Security Operations Center (SOC), email alerts frequently represent the highest volume of inbound user reports. An ad-hoc investigation method invites human error, creates blind spots around lateral movement, and risks executing live malicious payloads.

This 10-step workflow provides a deterministic, repeatable procedure for analyzing suspicious RFC 822 / RFC 5322 artifacts from initial receipt through manual containment.

Step 1: Secure Ingestion & Evidence Preservation

Never inspect suspicious emails directly inside an active desktop mail client. Export the message as a raw .eml or .msg byte stream. Compute an immediate SHA-256 hash of the artifact to establish a tamper-evident baseline before beginning any processing. 

sha256sum suspicious_sample.eml > sample_evidence.sha256

Step 2: Transport & MTA Hop Route Analysis

Parse the Received: header chain chronologically from bottom (origin client) to top (internal border gateway). Identify the connecting IP address of the first public SMTP hop (e.g., 198.51.100.42), resolve its Autonomous System Number (ASN), and inspect reverse DNS (PTR) records for bulletproof hosting or dynamic ISP pools.

Step 3: Cryptographic Authentication Evaluation

Inspect the perimeter gateway's Authentication-Results: header (RFC 8601). Check the SPF verdict on the envelope Return-Path, DKIM cryptographic signature validity, and DMARC alignment against the visible RFC 5322 From domain. Note whether ARC (RFC 8617) preserves authentication from an intermediate forwarder.

Step 4: Identity & Sender Header Triangulation

Cross-examine the three sender identities: Return-Path, From, and Reply-To. Look for classic BEC indicators, such as an executive display name paired with an external lookalike domain, or an internal corporate From address that routes replies to a free disposable webmail account.

Step 5: Content & Social Engineering Heuristics

Evaluate the body text for psychological manipulation patterns. High-risk indicators include artificial urgency ("account suspension within 2 hours"), wire transfer requests bypassing dual authorization, payroll deposit updates, and vendor bank account modifications.

Step 6: Hyperlink & Domain Inspection

Extract all embedded URLs without clicking them. Defang URLs (e.g., hxxps://login[.]example[.]com) before sharing. Inspect for Punycode IDN homoglyphs (e.g., Cyrillic 'а' replacing Latin 'a'), open redirects (e.g., OAuth or cloud storage URL wrappers), and newly registered top-level domains.

Step 7: Attachment & Payload Triaging

If attachments are present, compute their cryptographic hashes (SHA-256) and extract metadata. Flag dangerous extensions disguised by right-to-left override characters or double extensions (e.g., invoice.pdf.iso). Inspect HTML attachments for obfuscated JavaScript or SVG files used for credential smuggling.

Step 8: MITRE ATT&CK Matrix Mapping

Align the verified tactics to standard cybersecurity matrices:

  • T1566.001 (Spearphishing Attachment): Weaponized office documents or archive files.
  • T1566.002 (Spearphishing Link): Credential harvesting landing pages.
  • T1586.002 (Compromised Email Account): Internal spearphishing originating from a hijacked corporate mailbox.

Step 9: Blast Radius & Exposure Assessment

Query mail server logs (e.g., Google Workspace Admin audit log or Microsoft 365 Message Trace) using the subject line, sending IP, and Message-ID. Determine how many internal mailboxes received the identical lure and identify whether any users interacted with embedded links or submitted credentials.

Step 10: Manual Containment & Remediation

Execute defensive response actions across security tools (automated containment actions are not yet connected directly to external firewalls or identity providers, so manual coordination is required):

  • Perform an administrative search-and-purge to remove copies from all recipient inboxes.
  • Block malicious sender domains and destination IPs on network firewalls and secure web gateways.
  • If credentials were submitted, initiate password resets and revoke active OAuth tokens.
  • Brief the reporting user with positive feedback to encourage future threat reporting.
